Diffusion control of electrode reactions

When electron transfer is fast, the current is limited by how quickly the reactant reaches the surface. Everything below follows from one equation and one picture: a depletion layer that grows as the square root of time.

Try this. Watch the profile at 0.1 s and at 10 s. The layer thickens a hundredfold more slowly than the time passes, because δ grows with √t, and that single fact is why the current falls as 1/√t rather than exponentially. Then raise D and notice that both the layer and the current scale with √D, not with D. Warming the cell does the same thing indirectly: D is tied to temperature through the viscosity of water, so going from 25 to 60 °C roughly doubles D and raises the current by about 40%.

§1The physical process

Three mechanisms bring a species to an electrode: migration of charged species in the electric field, convection from stirring or density gradients, and diffusion down a concentration gradient. The Nernst–Planck equation collects them for one dimension:

Jj(x)=−Dj ∂Cj(x)∂x−zjFRTDjCj ∂ϕ(x)∂x+Cj v(x)J_j(x) = -D_j\,\frac{\partial C_j(x)}{\partial x} - \frac{z_jF}{RT}D_jC_j\,\frac{\partial \phi(x)}{\partial x} + C_j\,v(x)

Diffusion control means arranging the experiment so only the first term survives. Migration is suppressed with an excess of inert supporting electrolyte, which carries the current and collapses the field in solution. Convection is suppressed by working in quiescent solution, on a timescale short enough that density-driven flow has not yet developed, and long enough that charging of the double layer is over.

Equal mobilities assumed for all ions, 1:1 supporting electrolyte. The point is the order of magnitude, not the exact number.

§2Fick's laws and the growing layer

Fick's first law relates the flux to the gradient; combining it with mass conservation gives the second law, the equation at the top of this page. For a planar electrode in a quiet solution the boundary conditions are semi-infinite diffusion and a bulk concentration far away:

CO(x,0)=CO∗,lim⁡x→∞CO(x,t)=CO∗,CO(0,t)=0C_O(x,0) = C_O^{*}, \qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}C_O(x,t) = C_O^{*}, \qquad C_O(0,t) = 0

The last condition is the mass-transport-limited case: the potential is stepped far enough that every molecule reaching the surface reacts immediately. Laplace transformation then gives the profile and its surface gradient:

CO(x,t)=CO∗ erf ⁣(x2DOt),∂CO∂x∣x=0=CO∗πDOtC_O(x,t) = C_O^{*}\,\mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{D_Ot}}\right), \qquad \left.\frac{\partial C_O}{\partial x}\right|_{x=0} = \frac{C_O^{*}}{\sqrt{\pi D_O t}}

Multiplying the surface flux by nFA gives the Cottrell equation, and the gradient at the surface defines the equivalent linear layer δ = √(πDt), which is the thickness a straight-line profile would need to carry the same flux.

§3Limiting current and the Nernst diffusion layer

If the solution is stirred, or if the electrode is small enough that diffusion arrives from all directions, the depletion layer stops growing. The profile becomes linear across a layer of fixed thickness δ, and the current reaches a steady limiting value:

il=nFADOCO∗δi_l = \frac{nFAD_OC_O^{*}}{\delta}

The transient and the steady state meet where the growing layer reaches the hydrodynamic one, at t ≈ δ²/(πD). Before that time the experiment is a clean transient; after it, convection sets the answer. A microdisk of radius r reaches steady state by a different route, radial diffusion, with iss = 4nFD C* r, which is why microelectrodes give steady currents in unstirred solution.

B. Cyclic voltammetry, the Randles–Ševčík analysis

D. Impedance, the Warburg coefficient

In the Randles circuit, semi-infinite diffusion appears as the Warburg impedance, equal real and imaginary parts falling as ω−1/2. It produces the 45° line at low frequency in the Nyquist plot, and a straight line of slope σ when Z′ is plotted against ω−1/2:

ZW=σ ω−1/2(1−j),σ=RTn2F2A2(1DO1/2CO∗+1DR1/2CR∗)Z_W = \sigma\,\omega^{-1/2}(1-j), \qquad \sigma = \frac{RT}{n^{2}F^{2}A\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{D_O^{1/2}C_O^{*}} + \frac{1}{D_R^{1/2}C_R^{*}}\right)

With both forms of the couple present at the same concentration and with equal diffusion coefficients, this collapses to D = [√2 RT / (n²F²AσC*)]².

      §6Is it really diffusion control?

      Before using any of those equations, two questions have to be answered: is the species diffusing freely to the surface or already attached to it, and is the electron transfer fast enough on the timescale of the experiment?

      The first question is settled by the scan-rate dependence. A freely diffusing species gives ip ∝ v1/2; a surface-confined one gives ip ∝ v, because all of the material is already there and simply reacts faster as the scan quickens. A log–log plot answers it in one picture.

      ip(surface)=n2F2AΓv4RTi_p^{\text{(surface)}} = \frac{n^{2}F^{2}A\Gamma v}{4RT}

      The second is settled by the peak separation. For a reversible system ΔEp ≈ 59/n mV and does not change with v. As the scan rate outruns the electron transfer, ΔEp grows, and Nicholson's analysis converts it to a rate constant through the dimensionless parameter ψ, with the working curve usually taken in the fitted form of Lavagnini:

      ψ=k0(πDnFvRT)−1/2\psi = k^{0}\left(\frac{\pi D n F v}{RT}\right)^{-1/2}
      ψ≈−0.6288+0.0021 X1−0.017 X,X=ΔEp in mV\psi \approx \frac{-0.6288 + 0.0021\,X}{1 - 0.017\,X}, \qquad X = \Delta E_p \text{ in mV}

      §7Simulation sandbox

      The same diffusion equation solved numerically, in the explicit finite-difference form of Appendix B of Bard and Faulkner. Space is divided into boxes of width Δx and time into steps Δt, and the only parameter that matters is their combination, the model diffusion coefficient DM = DΔt/Δx². The surface box is held at the Nernstian ratio set by the step potential.

      Cjk+1=Cjk+DM(Cj+1k−2Cjk+Cj−1k),DM=D ΔtΔx2C_j^{k+1} = C_j^{k} + D_M\left(C_{j+1}^{k} - 2C_j^{k} + C_{j-1}^{k}\right), \qquad D_M = \frac{D\,\Delta t}{\Delta x^{2}}

      Above DM = 0.5 the scheme is unstable: the profile oscillates and then explodes. It is worth showing students that failure once, because it is the clearest demonstration that the grid is not free.

      §8Check your understanding

      A Cottrell experiment gives a straight i vs t−1/2 line with a large positive intercept. What is wrong?

      A genuine Cottrell line passes through the origin. A positive intercept usually means a current that does not decay: a parallel faradaic reaction, a leaking cell, or an adsorbed species contributing a time-independent term. Charging current does the opposite, inflating the early points and bending the line near large t−1/2.

      Your peak current doubles when the scan rate doubles. Can you use Randles–Ševčík to get D?

      No. Linearity with v, not with v1/2, indicates a surface-confined species. There is no diffusion coefficient to extract; what you can extract is the coverage Γ from the peak area.

      Two students report D for the same couple: 7.6 × 10⁻⁶ and 6.1 × 10⁻⁶ cm² s⁻¹. One measured the electrode diameter with a ruler. How much of the gap can that explain?

      All of it. D scales with A⁻², so an area off by 12% moves D by about 25%. On a 3 mm disk, reading the diameter as 3.0 instead of 2.8 mm does exactly that. Calibrate the area electrochemically with a couple of known D instead of trusting the nominal diameter.

      Why does the Cottrell current measured at 60 s sit above the ideal line, while the one at 1 ms sits below it?

      At long times natural convection delivers extra material, holding the layer thinner than √(πDt) predicts, so the current is higher than the ideal decay. At very short times the measured current is dominated by double-layer charging and by the cell time constant RuCdl, which cannot follow the step, so the faradaic part is incomplete.

      At what time does the diffusion layer reach 100 µm, and why does that number matter?

      From δ = √(πDt), with D = 7.6 × 10⁻⁶ cm² s⁻¹ it takes about 4 s. It matters because 100 µm is the order of the layer that mild stirring sustains: beyond that time the quiescent experiment is no longer quiescent.

      Pitfalls checklist

      • Supporting electrolyte at least fifty to a hundred times the analyte concentration, or migration is part of your answer.
      • Electrode area measured, not assumed. D carries the error squared.
      • Concentrations in mol cm⁻³ when using these equations: 5 mM is 5 × 10⁻⁶ mol cm⁻³.
      • Solution quiescent and thermostatted; a vibrating bench shows up as scatter at long times.
      • Oxygen removed if the potential window reaches its reduction.
      • iR compensation checked before any peak separation is interpreted.
      • Two independent techniques agreeing within 10% before a value is reported.
